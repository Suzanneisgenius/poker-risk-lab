Poker Risk Lab: Understanding Uncertainty in Simulation

How much should we trust a simulated risk estimate? This is an interactive study of how numerical error, uncertain inputs and modelling assumptions affect a bankroll-risk estimate. Poker is the setting; the question applies to any simulated risk with an absorbing boundary.

Xinrui Song · Updated 2026-11-01 · How the numbers are checked

The short answer

For one scenario — 30 buy-ins, a +3 bb/100 win rate, 90 bb/100 swings, 100,000 hands — the model's exact risk of ruin is 7.17%. A single simulation at the Risk Lab's default 2,000 paths lands within about ±1.13 points of it. Not knowing the win rate after 50,000 hands leaves anything from 0.1% to 83.3% plausible, and five ways results might arrive give 6.4% to 20.3%. More computing only fixes the first.

The study does three things. It measures simulation error, with repeated runs, interval coverage, and checks against a closed-form value and independent implementations. It propagates uncertainty about the win rate, pushing a posterior through the risk formula instead of plugging in one number. And it stress-tests the assumptions: heavy tails, rare shocks and dependence at the same edge show which departures matter. Sections 1 to 3 below take these in turn.

A lower risk that was not a real change

After an engine update, the same settings and seed gave a lower risk: 7.85% → 6.35%. Two things had changed at once, the random numbers and how ruin is monitored, so each was switched on separately and then rerun over 40 seeds. The drop came from drawing different random numbers. The new monitoring raised risk on every seed and brought the average close to the exact value.

Which players are you compared with? The prior N(0, 10²) is an assumption. Real hands put true win rates much closer together. In PartyPoker and Ongame no-limit hold'em, $2/$4, 1–23 July 2009, HandHQ logs, the 175 regulars with 10,000+ hands had true win rates averaging +2.6 bb/100 with a standard deviation of 2.5 (at most 3.5). Across all 1,347 players with 1,000+ hands they averaged −1.2, with a standard deviation of 5.3. The assumed 10 is about 4 times the regulars' spread, and a centre of 0 is not neutral: the average seat lost the rake, 3.8 to 4.6 bb/100.

With few hands, the comparison group moves the answer more than the results do: after 10,000 hands the median risk is 8.7% with the regulars' prior and 23.0% with the all-players prior (14.7% with N(0, 10²)). By 1,000,000 hands the three agree, at 8.0% to 8.5% average risk.

Only the regulars' estimate is clean. Players with fewer hands did 4.7 to 9.7 bb/100 worse in the later half of their hands than in the earlier half, as if many stopped after a losing run; the regulars changed by −0.2 (−1.8 to +1.6). Predicting each regular's later half from the earlier half, the fitted prior cut the squared error to 0.54 of using the earlier half as it is (N(0, 10²): 0.63), and its 95% intervals covered 95.4% of results (N(0, 10²): 97.7%, too wide). Try both priors on your own results: the Bankroll Risk tool computes them on the server as you change the inputs.

Method. Suppose a player's results so far average exactly +3 bb/100. The win rate's standard error is 90 / √(hands / 100); a Normal posterior (weakly informative prior, N(0, 10²)) is pushed through the risk formula with 20,000 draws. Simulation intervals are binomial 95% ranges at the same win rate. The priors from real hands come from a hierarchical Normal model fitted within four groups of players by hands played. The all-players prior is the mixture of the groups, weighted by players; its posterior reweights them by how well each predicts the observed win rate. Every prior uses the same draws and seed. Real-hand intervals: 95%, resampling players (2,000 bootstrap samples). The live tool computes the same posteriors in closed form, and checks/bankroll-posterior-python/ compares them with this code.

Limits. The swing size is treated as known, and results are assumed independent; both make these intervals narrower than a full analysis would. The priors from real hands describe one stake on two sites in July 2009, after rake. “Regulars” means 10,000+ hands in those 23 days, not over a career. The all-players centre depends on when players stopped, so treat it as approximate. The fit was made outside this repository, which records only its summary numbers (configs/population_priors.json). Data: PHH dataset, Kim (2024), Recording and Describing Poker Hands, arXiv:2312.11753, CC BY 4.0.

3 If results are not independent or not Normal, is the estimate still reliable?

Details for

Same edge everywhere. Changing the shape of a 100-hand result barely moves risk (6.4% with heavy tails, 7.3% with rare shocks, against 7.0%). Letting swings carry over from one step to the next raises it to 20.3% (×2.87): each step still has the same spread, but the 100,000-hand total varies 1.37× as much.

The dependence, or the wider total? Narrow each step until the 100,000-hand total varies exactly as much as the baseline's, and risk falls back to 6.8% with carry-over and 7.1% with regimes. Almost all of the rise came from the wider total, so one number predicts the risk: the long-run spread, how much the total varies per 100 hands. Put it into the Brownian formula in place of 90 and it gives 21.2% for carry-over and 12.0% for regimes, close to the simulated 20.3% and 11.7%.

Fitted to real hands. In 45,367 blocks of 100 hands from 355 players with at least 5,000 hands each (PartyPoker and Ongame no-limit hold'em, $2/$4, 1–23 July 2009, HandHQ logs), a player's results were only mildly heavy-tailed: Student-t with 7.0 degrees of freedom (95% interval 6.5–7.6). Neighbouring blocks did not carry over (correlation −0.002, interval −0.011 to 0.007), and the total over 5,000 hands varied as much as independent blocks give (ratio 0.97, 0.92–1.03). With those settings risk is 6.7%, and 7.3% at the top of the correlation interval: within 0.32 points of the baseline. The carry-over and regime settings above are stress tests, far stronger than anything in these data.

Method. 40,000 paths per model, one seed. All models are compared using ruin observed at 100-hand checkpoints; the bridge correction is exact only for Normal steps, so it is not used here. This holds the observation grid fixed. It does not establish equal monitoring bias across models or determine their continuously monitored ruin probabilities. Intervals are Wilson 95%. The long-run spread comes from the exact variance of each model's 100,000-hand total. The formula is the finite-horizon Brownian first passage; it checks ruin continuously, so it sits a little above the checkpoints even for the baseline (7.2% against 7.0%). Real-hand intervals: 95%, resampling players (2,000 bootstrap samples).

Limits. The stress tests are controlled scenarios, not estimates of how real results arrive. The regime model's steps are also wider (standard deviation about 101 instead of 90). The formula needs the horizon to span many swings: with φ 0.9 over 10,000 hands and 10 buy-ins it gives 77.1% against a simulated 55.9% (baseline at those settings: 17.8% against 16.1%). The real-hand fit covers one stake on two sites in one month of 2009, and only regular players; it was made outside this repository, which records only its summary numbers (configs/case_study/assumptions.yaml). Data: PHH dataset, Kim (2024), Recording and Describing Poker Hands, arXiv:2312.11753, CC BY 4.0.

Checking a misleading result

After the simulator changed, the same settings and seed gave a lower risk at +3 bb/100: 7.85% → 6.35%. A lower risk is easy to read as an improvement. Two things had changed at once — how ruin is monitored and how random numbers are drawn — so each was switched on separately.

  1. Fix everything else. Same inputs, same seed, called directly through the calculation function.
  2. Change one thing at a time. New random numbers alone: 7.85% → 6.00% (−1.85 points, a different random sample). Adding the bridge correction: → 6.35% (+0.35 points).
  3. Look beyond one seed. Over seeds 0–39 the correction raised risk for every seed, by 0.355 points on average, and brought the average to 7.12% against the exact 7.17%.
  4. Conclusion. The drop was sampling, not a change in risk; the correction itself pushes the number up, as it should. The release notes say so, and every saved result now records the engine version.

Reproduce

Each figure on this page comes from one command and one configuration file. The outputs record the commit, seeds, path counts and software versions; this page is built from them by npm run build.

Install with pip install -r requirements-research.txt. Validation checks have their own commands on the Validation page.

What this does not show

  • That any of these models describes real poker results in full. Agreement with the exact value checks the arithmetic, not the model; the real-hand fits cover one stake on two sites in 2009: section 3 checks only the shape and carry-over of 100-hand results, and section 2 how far apart players' win rates are.
  • A recommended bankroll. Every number is a conditional model estimate, not financial or gambling advice.
  • Anything about the Preflop ranges, which are illustrative demonstration data.
